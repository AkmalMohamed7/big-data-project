Rand ORCID: orcid.org/0000-0001-8975-2783 1 , 5 , 7 , 8 Show authors Nature volume 648 , pages 394–401 ( 2025 ) Cite this article 16k Accesses 19 Citations 822 Altmetric Metrics details Subjects Politics Psychology Social sciences Abstract There is great public concern about the potential use of generative artificial intelligence (AI) for political persuasion and the resulting impacts on elections and democracy 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 . We inform these concerns using pre-registered experiments to assess the ability of large language models to influence voter attitudes. In the context of the 2024 US presidential election, the 2025 Canadian federal election and the 2025 Polish presidential election, we assigned participants randomly to have a conversation with an AI model that advocated for one of the top two candidates. We observed significant treatment effects on candidate preference that are larger than typically observed from traditional video advertisements 7 , 8 , 9 . We also document large persuasion effects on Massachusetts residents’ support for a ballot measure legalizing psychedelics. Examining the persuasion strategies 9 used by the models indicates that they persuade with relevant facts and evidence, rather than using sophisticated psychological persuasion techniques. Not all facts and evidence presented, however, were accurate; across all three countries, the AI models advocating for candidates on the political right made more inaccurate claims. Together, these findings highlight the potential for AI to influence voters and the important role it might play in future elections. (A) Distribution of pre-treatment candidate preference by party in our sample. Partisans entered the study with highly polarized views of the candidates. Thus, there was limited room for the pro-Harris AI to persuade Harris supporters or the pro-Trump AI to persuade Trump supporters. (B) Participants were randomized to indicate either the candidate’s personal characteristic or policy issue that was the most important to them (selected from pre-determined lists of 19 personal characteristics and 19 policy issues). Here we show the fraction of Harris and Trump supporters selecting each option (the top 10 are shown, while the remaining 9 are collapsed into the “Other” category). We see marked differences between Harris and Trump supporters in terms of their top-priority personality characteristics and policy issues. This heterogeneity emphasizes the potential value of tailored persuasion via AI dialogues. Extended Data Fig. 4 The AI conversations changed participants’ voting likelihood and vote choice in the U.S. presidential election experiment. (A) Shown is the pre-to-post change in voting likelihood (0–100 scale) by randomly assigned conditions and participants’ pre-treatment voting likelihood (median split into <100 versus 100) (preregistered analysis). Both pro-Harris and pro-Trump treatments increased voting intentions among in-party participants whose pre-treatment voting intention was not already at ceiling (100%). The pre-to-post decrease in voting likelihood among out-party participants initially at ceiling likely reflect regression to the mean (as they can only down from 100%). (B) Shown is the pre-to-post change in vote choice (categorical outcome based on who participants report they would vote for if the election happened today) ( post hoc analysis). The left half of each panel shows the fraction of people choosing Harris, whereas the right half of each panel shows the fraction of people choosing Trump. The pro-Harris AI and pro-Trump AI increased the probability of voting for Harris versus Trump, respectively. Error bars indicate 95% confidence intervals of the mean. See SI Section 2.1 for details. Extended Data Fig. 7 Causal forest conditional average treatment effects (CATE) on candidate preference in the U.S. presidential election experiment ( post hoc analysis). Shown are the partial dependence plots for pre-treatment covariates that are considered important (i.e., weighted sum of how many times a feature was split on at each depth in the causal forest) for predicting post-treatment candidate preference in the training set, ordered left to right by importance. A covariate is considered “important” if its importance value is higher than the mean importance across all covariates. The CATEs are estimated on a holdout testing dataset; for each covariate, we hold all other covariates at their median values. For example, pre-treatment candidate preference has an inverted-U relationship with post-treatment candidate preference, such that more moderate participants (with no strong preference for Harris [0] or Trump [100]) show stronger treatment effects. Participants with higher political interest and involvement have weaker (but still positive) treatment effects. Error bars indicate 95% confidence intervals of the mean. Pre-treatment covariates used for model fitting: candidate preference, voting likelihood, conversation focus (personality or policy), age, income, education, belief in God, partisanship, political orientation, social conservatism, economic conservatism, area lived in, gender, ethnicity, general trust in AI, political interest, political involvement, warmth toward Democrats (feeling thermometer), warmth toward Republicans, importance of considering policy issue, importance of considering personality, policy versus personality consideration importance (difference). Extended Data Fig. 8 The AI models used different persuasion strategies in the Canadian federal election experiment. Shown is the average use of 27 strategies across conversations. For each conversation, two different AI models rated the use of each strategy using a 3-level scale (0, 0.5, 1) and those two ratings were then averaged. The AI model in the no-facts condition used much less evidence and facts than the AI model in the baseline condition. Error bars indicate 95% confidence intervals of the mean. Extended Data Fig. 9 The AI models used different persuasion strategies in the Polish presidential election experiment. Shown is the average use of 27 strategies across conversations. For each conversation, two different AI models rated the use of each strategy using a 3-level scale (0, 0.5, 1) and those two ratings were then averaged. The AI model in the no-facts condition used much less evidence and facts than the AI model in the baseline condition. The AI model in the non-personalized condition did not personalize its responses to the participants as much. Error bars indicate 95% confidence intervals of the mean. Extended Data Fig. 10 AI model statement accuracy varied across models and experimental conditions (exploratory post hoc robustness check). Shown are the distributions with means and 95% CIs of accuracy ratings for simulated statements generated by 12 open-source and proprietary AI models for the candidate preference experiment. For all 12 models, the pro-Trump AI made more inaccurate statements than the pro-Harris AI, particularly when discussing personal characteristics. The accuracy ratings were determined by Perplexity, a large language model that can access real-time information from the internet. Error bars indicate 95% confidence intervals of the mean.
